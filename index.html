01 · The system

What is ROMO?

Before you explore any prototypes, it helps to understand what this system is — and what it gently asks of you.

ROMO is a behavioural interaction system — not a productivity app, not an organiser, not a to-do list. It is designed to help people mindfully revisit and release saved digital content, instead of endlessly accumulating it.

Every day we save articles, videos, posts, screenshots, and links across dozens of platforms. Most of it is never opened again. It sits there — growing heavier, quietly creating guilt, cognitive load, and a persistent sense of falling behind. ROMO asks a different question: what if you could let go?

The system has two parts that work together: a companion physical device built on an ESP32 microcontroller with a small monochrome LCD screen, and a mobile app that connects to your saved content. Together, they form a calm ecosystem for revisiting what you've saved — and deciding, with intention, whether it still matters to you.

Physical device

A small monochrome companion object — always visible near your desk or bedside. It re-surfaces saved content on a 128×64 LCD screen using a rotary encoder for slow, tactile interaction.

Mobile app

Connects to your saved content across platforms. Transfers items into the ROMO ecosystem and provides a calm mobile interface for reviewing, reflecting on, and releasing content.

Reflective interactions

Every interaction is designed to slow you down — intentional friction, calm animation, and emotional pacing replace the instant swipe-and-scroll patterns of conventional apps.

Release rituals

When you decide to let go, ROMO does not just delete. It offers release interactions — burning, dissolving, fading, peeling away — that give emotional closure to the act of letting go.

03 · Context

Why we save everything
but revisit almost nothing

Understanding the emotional problem ROMO responds to.

We save content compulsively — bookmarks, screenshots, posts, articles, videos — not because we will return to it, but because letting go feels like losing something. The save button has become an emotional reflex, a promise we make to our future selves that we rarely keep.

Over time, this accumulation creates cognitive overload: a quiet, persistent weight. The growing list of unwatched, unread, unfinished content becomes a source of guilt rather than inspiration. The fear of missing out (FOMO) that drove the saving transforms into a different anxiety — one rooted in abundance rather than scarcity.

Deleting feels difficult because saved items carry emotional charge — they represent aspirations, moods, moments of curiosity. They feel like fragments of who we wanted to become. Discarding them can feel like abandoning those possibilities.

ROMO proposes a different path: instead of organizing or optimizing this behaviour, it explores mindful release — giving people calm, reflective, emotionally safe ways to let go of digital content that no longer serves them.

Attention economy

Platforms design for maximum capture. Every save button is an engagement metric. Your backlog is their retention strategy.

Emotional weight

Saved content is not neutral data. It carries aspiration, guilt, nostalgia, and unfinished intention — a quiet psychological burden.

The deletion barrier

We hesitate to delete because loss aversion is powerful. Content feels scarce even when it is infinitely reproducible.

Calm technology

ROMO draws from Weiser and Brown's calm technology principles: technology should inform without demanding attention.

04 · How to test

Before you begin,
slow down

This is not a usability test in the traditional sense. You are not looking for bugs or efficiency. You are observing how each prototype makes you feel.

01

Explore slowly

Open each prototype one at a time. Spend at least 30 seconds with each before forming an opinion. Notice what draws your attention and what your hands want to do.

02

Feel the atmosphere

Pay attention to visual warmth, pacing, silence, and emotional texture. Does the interface feel heavy or light? Anxious or calm? Clinical or intimate?

03

Notice the release moment

Each prototype includes a letting-go interaction. Observe your emotional response. Does it feel satisfying? Painful? Relieving? Ceremonial? Gentle? Abrupt?

04

Think about the physical

Imagine these interfaces on a small monochrome device near your desk or bedside. Would you reach for it? Would it feel like a companion or a chore?

05

Give feedback immediately

Each prototype has a short feedback form directly beneath it. Fill it in while the feeling is still fresh — do not wait until the end.

05B · Device prototypes

ROMO device
interaction prototypes

Six interactive prototypes exploring different approaches to how users revisit, preview, and release saved content on the physical ROMO device.

Prototype 01 · Earliest concept

Auto-Capture Shuffle

This was the earliest ROMO concept. The system automatically detected saved content and generated screenshots that fell into the device automatically. The user clicked the device button to shuffle through forgotten content randomly.

This concept was not successful — users had little control and automatic screenshot collection felt disconnected from intentional emotional interaction.

Observe & feel
  • Lack of control
  • Random vs intentional
  • Automated feeling
  • Emotional disconnect

Prototype 02 · Manual send + sticker peel

Peel-Away Release

Users manually send saved content to the ROMO device. Content appears again later at random emotional moments. Users can preview the title and saved time, then decide: let go or go back. If released, the content peels away like a sticker and disappears.

Prototype 03 · Countdown tension

Timer Explosion

Saved content receives a visible countdown timer. As the timer approaches zero, the content vibrates and tension increases. Users can release, refresh, or close. If time expires, the content explodes and disappears automatically — the system decides for you.

Prototype 04 · Ceremonial destruction

Hold to Burn

Content scrolls horizontally. Users select content and preview it. A click goes back; holding the button burns the content — a ceremonial destruction interaction that transforms deletion into an emotional ritual.

Category + Gunshot Release
